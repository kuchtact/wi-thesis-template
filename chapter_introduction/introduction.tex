Magnetic field and plasmas coexist in space and on earth in many forms \cite{Zweibel_Yamada_2016}. In these systems, field lines can undergo topological rearrangement because of magnetic reconnection converting magnetic energy into plasma energy, leading to hotter flowing plasmas and energetic particles \cite{Yamada_Kulsrud_Ji_2010}.

Magnetic reconnection plays a large part in geomagnetic storms that are driven by the interplay of the Earth's magnetosphere and the Sun's explosive plasma releases. These storms can cause power outages, start fires, and render satellites useless. Additionally, the initializers of these storms (coronal mass ejections and solar flares) require magnetic reconnection that allows the Sun's plasma to escape from the Sun with immense energy. The mechanisms that drive these various components require an understanding of reconnection. A comprehensive review of magnetic reconnection can be found in \cite{Yamada_Kulsrud_Ji_2010} and \cite{Zweibel_Yamada_2009}.

\section{Reconnection Theory}

There have been many different theories used to understand the breaking of field lines where each depends on a different combination of terms to break Ohm's law (i.e. $\vb{E} + \vb{v} \cross \vb{B} \neq 0$).

\subsection{The Generalized Ohm's Law}

The idealized Ohm's law as seen in ideal magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) is
\begin{equation}
  \vb{E} + \vb{v} \cross \vb{B} = 0,
\end{equation}
where $\vb{E}$ is the electric field, $\vb{v}$ is the plasma flow velocity, and $\vb{B}$ is the magnetic field. This condition forces the field lines to evolve differentiably and thus magnetic reconnection can't occur. Ideal MHD assumes that collisions between particles do not occur, different species in the plasma act as fluids that move together, and particle distribution functions are always Maxwellian. In the aforementioned physical systems, each of these assumptions (amongst other assumptions) may break leading to the the idealized Ohm's law no longer describing the system.

Using the electron momentum equation we can derive the following generalized Ohm's law:
\begin{equation}
  \vb{E} + \vb{u} \cross \vb{B} = \eta \vb{J} + \frac{\vb{J}\cross\vb{B}}{ne} - \frac{\nabla \cdot\vb{P}_e}{ne}-\frac{m_e}{e}\dv{\vb{u}_e}{t},
  \label{eq:generalized_ohms_law}
\end{equation} % TODO: what are other components that might go into the generalized Ohm's law (relativistic effects? ...?)
where $\vb{u}$ is the plasma flow velocity, $\eta$ is the resistivity of the plasma, $\vb{J} = ne(\vb{u}_i + \vb{u}_e)$ is the current density, $n$ is the number density, $e$ is the elementary charge, $\vb{P}_e$ is the electron pressure tensor, $m_e$ is the electron mass, and $\vb{u}_e$ ($\vb{u}_i$) is the electron (ion) flow velocity \cite{Zweibel_Yamada_2016}.

\subsubsection{Plasma Resistance}

The first term on the right hand side, $\eta \vb{J}$, causes the breaking of Ohm's law via the plasma resistance. This term is important when the reconnection stays MHDlike and is important in Sweet-Parker reconnection which is characterized by exceptionally slow reconnection rates, $R$, where the rate goes as
\begin{equation}
  R = \frac{v_\text{in}}{v_\text{out}} = \sqrt{S},
\end{equation}
where $v_\text{in}$ and $v_\text{out}$ is the inflow and outflow speeds and $S$ is the Lundquist number. The Lundquist number is a ratio of the speed at a field line convects divided by how quickly it diffuses. This model underpredicts the reconnection rate for events occurring in our solar system (e.g. Sweet-Parker predicts that solar flares should reconnect in months whereas they actually reconnect in minutes to hours).

\subsubsection{Separation of Ions and Electrons}
\label{ss2:hall_reconnection}

The second term, $(\vb{J} \cross \vb{B}) / ne$, is the Hall term. This term appears when the electrons and ions can decouple at scale lengths of the ion skin depth, $d_i$. When the current sheet is on the order of a few $d_i$ or less, the ions become demagnetized and thus decouple from the magnetic field crossing from the inflow to the outflow and thus have separate dynamics than the electrons which stay magnetized. This causes additional currents that create a quadrupolar out of plane field structure near the x-point (see \cite{Zweibel_Yamada_2009}). This term can't actually break field lines because at the x-point the reconnecting field component drops to zero and since the current is out of the plane this leads to $(\vb{J} \cross \vb{B}) / ne = 0$.

\subsubsection{Kinetic Physics}

At scales of the electron skin depth, the electron pressure anisotropy and electron inertia terms can come into play (the right two terms of the generalized Ohm's law). % TODO: Is this true.

\paragraph{Electron Inertia}

If the two inflows have different plasma parameters this can lead to the electron inertia term (the rightmost of equation \ref{eq:generalized_ohms_law}) breaking Ohm's law. In this case, the location where $B$ flips sign (the x-point) is not at the same location as where the plasma flows balance. Thus after expanding the convective derivative we find
\begin{equation}
  \dv{\vb{u}}{t} = \pdv{\vb{u}}{t} + \vb{u}\cdot \nabla \vb{u}.
\end{equation}
Assuming the system is in equilibrium (by this we mean it is time translation invariant for bulk quantities yet the field lines are still able to move) the partial derivative drops out. This equilibrium condition forces a pressure balance and thus a relation between $n$ and $B$ between them. Since the plasma flow is non-zero at the x-point the second term can be non-zero.

\paragraph{Electron Pressure Anisotropy}

If the plasma is sufficiently collisionless, the distribution function can become non-Maxwellian leading to a breaking of the frozen-in condition which is well derived in \cite{Egedal_2023}. Only the off diagonal terms of the pressure tensor lead to breaking of the Ohm's law which appear because of meandering orbits across the x-point and trapped particles in the inflows. % TODO: Check this.

\subsubsection{Summary}

Each term on the right side of the generalized Ohm's law has led to lots of exciting research and each term balances to a varying extent depending on two scaling parameters, the system size defined by $L / d_i$ and the Lundquist number, $S$. A discussion of these regimes can be found in any of the aforementioned theses. In this work we are looking reconnection where pressure anisotropy plays a large part.

\subsection{Electron Pressure Anisotropy}

\subsubsection{Distribution Function}
\label{ss2:intro_PA_what}

In a fluid understanding of a plasma, the pressure is often isotropic where only a single scalar variable, $p_e = nT_e$, is needed to define the electron plasma pressure. The plasma distribution in these fluid approximations is a Maxwellian which has rotational symmetry (once the flow velocity has been removed) which is given by
\begin{equation}
  f(\vb{v}) = \left(\frac{e m_e}{2 \pi}\right)^{3/2} \frac{n}{T^{3/2}} \exp \left[-\frac{e m_e}{2} \frac{(\vb{v} - \vb{v}_D)^2}{T}\right].
  \label{eq:maxwellian_distribution}
\end{equation}
Fluid models with an isotropic pressure closure enforce this requirement but this is not necessarily a good representation of the plasma when collisions are not as dominant. With low collisionality the distribution can become non-Maxwellian and to the next order, the distribution can be represented via a bi-Maxwellian where the temperature parallel to the magnetic field is different than the temperature perpendicular. This leads to a parallel and perpendicular pressure given by $n T_\parallel$ and $n T_\perp$ which we will from here on out call \textit{pressure anisotropy}.

There can be higher order effects that lead to either off-diagonal terms in the pressure tensor or non-gyrotropic distributions where there isn't just one perpendicular temperature. This will be discussed more in the chapter on the pressure anisotropy probe and the physics effects of off diagonal terms can be learned about in \cite{Egedal_2023}.

\subsubsection{Anisotropy Generation}
\label{ss2:intro_PA_generation}

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_introduction/figures/flux_tube_evolution.png}
  \caption{This figure shows an example reconnection configuration on the scale of the ion skin depth for collisionless reconnection. The green arrows show the direction of flow into the x-point. Electrons trapped in a flux tube far from the x-point (with distribution function in panel A) are convected towards a region of lower field causing the flux tube to widen vertically (panel B). To enforce quasineutrality this requires the tubes to shorten in the parallel direction. The passing electrons (in green and purple which originate from a Maxwellian distribution) move to higher $v_\parallel$ whereas trapped electrons get smeared out causing the distribution to have an increasingly large $T_\parallel$ and smaller $T_\perp$.}
  \label{fig:flux_tube_evolution}
\end{figure}

The electron distribution function does not necessarily need to be anisotropic far from the x-point and often, due to collisions, starts as an isotropic Maxwellian. Anisotropy instead forms as the flux tubes travel towards the x-point once inside the ion diffusion region.

% TODO: Why does the parallel electric field come into play? Le et al. 2010 Magnitude of ..., Le et al. 2010 Equations of state ...

In the diffusion region, ions are decoupled from electron dynamics and have a nearly uniform number density, $n_i$. The electrons split into two populations along each field line, those with passing orbits which can travel along the entire field line and others that are trapped due to two effects \cite{Le_Egedal_Fox_Katz_Vrublevskis_Daughton_Drake_2010}; magnetic trapping due to conservation of magnetic moment where the magnetic field is smaller near the x-point compared to a distance along the field line; trapping due to a parallel electric field that forms along the field lines. % Is the reason for the electric field because the flux tube expansion is too much for magnetic trapping to sufficiently increase the electron particle number so there must be electric field trapping?
Assuming no collisions and using Liouville's theorem, we can construct
\begin{align}
  f(\vb{x}, \mathcal{E}_\parallel, \mathcal{E}_\perp) &= \\
  f(\vb{x}, \mathcal{E}_\parallel, \mu B(\vb{x})) &= \begin{cases}
    f_\infty\left(\mathcal{E}_\parallel - \mu B_\infty - e \Phi_\parallel, \mu B_\infty \right) & \text{passing} \\
    f_\infty\left(0, \mu B_\infty \right) & \text{trapped} \\
  \end{cases},
\end{align}
which defines the distribution function at all points of the inflow defined by the distribution function at infinity \cite{Le_Egedal_Daughton_Fox_Katz_2009}. $\mathcal{E}$ is the kinetic energy, $\mu$ is the magnetic moment, $B_\infty$ is the magnetic field at infinity, and $\Phi_\parallel$ is the electrostatic potential calculated via
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_\parallel(\vb{x}) = \int_{\vb{x}}^\infty \vb{E} \cdot \hat{b} \dd{\vb{x}'}.
\end{equation}
This can be simplified to the Chew, Goldberg, and Low (CGL) scaling for a flux tube with no heat heat fluxes (i.e. the passing particles are ignored and the distribution starts as a trapped Maxwellian in the a flux tube far from the x-point) \cite{Chew_Goldberger_Low_1956}
\begin{align}
  \frac{p_\parallel}{ p_\infty} &= \frac{\pi}{6} \left(\frac{n}{n_\infty}\right)^3 \left(\frac{B}{B_\infty}\right)^{-2} \label{eq:cgl_parallel} \\
  \frac{p_\perp}{ p_\infty} &= \frac{n}{n_\infty} \frac{B}{B_\infty}. \label{eq:cgl_perp}
\end{align}
Thus as the flux tube convects inwards and the magnetic field drops, the parallel pressure rises while the perpendicular pressure falls leading to pressure anisotropy just before the plasma enters the x-point. This same derivation and a review of these effects can be seen in \cite{Egedal_Le_Daughton_2013}.

\paragraph{Effect of guide field}

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_introduction/figures/field_line_curvature.png}
  \caption{The left panel shows an example reconnection region with a single field line in the outflow identified in red. The guide field is in the $\hat{\phi}$ direction. For low guide field (top right), the field line has a very small radius of curvature, $R_B$, since the field line is sharply bent and thus $\rho_e \gg R_B$. In this case the electron does not conserve magnetic moment and the distribution function is isotropized. In the case of high guide field (bottom right), the electron gyroradius is small relative to the radius of curvature and the pressure anisotropy is conserved.}
  \label{fig:field_line_curvature}
\end{figure}

A guide field is a magnetic field out of the page in figure \ref{fig:field_line_curvature} that does not reconnect. This guide field can be present to varying extents in any reconnecting system but changes the effects of pressure anisotropy.

\subparagraph{Low field}

If we have a very small guide field, the magnitude of the magnetic field changes a lot between the initial field, $B_\infty$, and the field near the x-point. In the extreme case of no guide field, the magnitude of the field is zero at the x-point and thus the CGL predict infinite parallel pressure! Fortunately finite gyroradius effects save us from this catastrophe as the gyroradius increases for decreasing field strength. Although the plasma does have a large amount of pressure anisotropy the distribution isotropizes near the x-point because the of a lack of magnetic moment conservation caused by a very small radius of curvature of the reconnecting field line \cite{Le_Egedal_Ohia_Daughton_Karimabadi_Lukin_2013}.

\subparagraph{High field}

In the case of a very large guide field, the pressure anisotropy is conserved into the outflow but there isn't much pressure anisotropy to begin with! This is because the magnitude of the magnetic field does not change very much as the field line convects inwards leading to not much change in the flux tube shape and thus minimal pressure anisotropy.

\subparagraph{Moderate field}

In the case of a moderate guide field, the pressure anisotropy grows in the inflow and is conserved in the outflow. In this case there is sufficient anisotropy to change the outflow behavior.

\subsubsection{Effects of pressure anisotropy}

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_introduction/figures/low_v_high_field_pa_le_2013.png}
  \caption{PIC simulations of reconnection from \cite{Le_Egedal_Ohia_Daughton_Karimabadi_Lukin_2013}. The top row shows a moderate guide field run and the bottom row a low guide field run. With sufficiently high field, the pressure anisotropy in panel (a) stays in the outflow as compared to the low guide field. This leads to a marginal firehose condition of order $0.5$ (panel (b)) in the outflow which supports sharply bent field lines and a current jet in the center of the outflow (panel (c)).}
\end{figure}

If the pressure anisotropy is preserved after flowing through the x-point, it can balance the magnetic tension force allowing the field lines to be sharply bent. This requires a large amount of anisotropy which is not generated in high guide field reconnection. At this sharply bent field line, the out of plane current is peaked which lies between both separatrices. The anisotropy required to balance the tension is given by the marginal firehose condition,
\begin{equation}
  F = \frac{\mu_0 (p_\parallel - p_\perp)}{B^2}.
\end{equation}
This is derived by balancing the diagonal terms of the plasma pressure tensor with those of the Maxwell stress tensor such that the total pressure is isotropic:
\begin{align*}
  (\bm{\sigma} - \vb{P})_{ii} &= C \\
  \left(\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} \begin{bmatrix}
    1 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & -1 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & -1 \\
  \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}
    p_\parallel & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & p_\perp & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & p_\perp \\
  \end{bmatrix}\right)_{ii} &= \\
  \Rightarrow -\frac{B^2}{2 \mu_0} + p_\parallel &= \frac{B^2}{2 \mu_0} + p_\perp \\
  \Rightarrow F \coloneq \frac{\mu_0 (p_\parallel - p_\perp)}{B^2} &= 1.
\end{align*}

\subsubsection{Necessity of Terrestrial Experiments}

Both spacecraft and simulation have identified pressure anisotropy during reconnection in the magnetosphere and the effects on the reconnection dynamics, while understood theoretically, have not yet been measured in a terrestrial experiment. Spacecraft work well to measure the actual dynamics in the magnetosphere but have minimal control over when they measure reconnection near the x-point and and where they pass through the reconnection region. Additionally they are very expensive to create and maintain while also lacking the ability to do controllable experiments. Simulations on the other hand are comparatively far cheaper and very controllable. They also measure every parameter in the reconnecting system but because of the assumptions required to run are not necessarily physical. Terrestrial experiments bridge both these options by being perfectly physical but still have assumptions that space-like conditions can be scaled down to the laboratory. They are often reproducible and external fields and plasmas can be controlled by the operator while being far cheaper than a spacecraft. Unfortunately laboratory experiments can only measure targeted plasma parameters and thus diagnostics must be developed for plasma parameters and conditions of interest.

A diagram of the experiment can be seen in figure \ref{fig:brb_cad_rendering} with timescales given in \ref{fig:experimental_sequence_timeline}. The experiment begins with an applied background field creating a nearly uniform field, $\vb{B}_H(r, z) \approx B_H \hat{z}$ (section 2.6 of \cite{Olson_2020}). We then inject plasma using a set of washer guns (section 2.3.2 of \cite{Olson_2020}) along the $z$ axis which diffuses radially outwards creating a peaked density profile. At this time we can also turn on a toroidal field coil that consists of seven wires running along the $z$ axis which give a radially variable guide field for reconnection. From here we pulse either a three coil system with coils at $z \in \{-15, 15, 45\}$ cm (as described in section 4.2 of \cite{Greess}) or a drive cylinder (as seen in \cite{Gradney_Egedal_Barnhill_Flores-García_Greess_Kuchta_Olson_Wallace_Yu_Forest_2023}).

These coils/cylinder generate a reversed magnetic field creating a current sheet in the plasma where beneath the current sheet, the plasma is frozen into the Helmholtz field and above the sheet, is frozen into the drive field. This current layer moves radially inwards as seen in figure \ref{fig:flux_array_movie_frames}.

\subsection{Diagnostics Overview}
\label{sss:diagnostics_overview}

All the data in this thesis comes from four probes. Two of the probes, the multi-tip langmuir probe and pressure anisotropy probe, have chapters devoted to them in this thesis (\ref{sch:multitip_probe} and \ref{sch:pa_probe}). These are both used for measuring the densities, temperatures, and plasma potential distributions in the system. We also use two other probes for measuring the changes in the magnetic field. A linear array of B-dot probes measures the three components of B-dot at a single $r$ and $\phi$ coordinate but along many $z$ positions. For this probe, the layer passes the probe and the configuration can be reconstructed via the jogging method (section 5.3 of \cite{Greess}). The other is a set of flux arrays which consist of many B-dot probes in a plane normal to $\hat{\phi}$. This probe can create a movie of the evolving magnetic field for each shot and is described in section A.2 of \cite{Gradney_2025}.

\section{Thesis Outline}

This thesis is oriented around developing a variety of techniques for measuring plasma parameter at high resolution during reconnection. We begin with a chapter on the use of pressure waves that propagate at the start of out experiment to determine the initial plasma profile. From here, we develop theories for our multi-tip Langmuir probes such that we can extract more accurate results. Finally, we end with a discussion of the pressure anisotropy probe, a probe used for measuring the pressure anisotropy during reconnection that has not previously been observed in the laboratory.
