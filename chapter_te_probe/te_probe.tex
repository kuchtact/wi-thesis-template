\section{Langmuir probes}
\label{sse:multitip_langmuir_what}

There exist a wide variety of plasma diagnostics that depend on measurable plasma parameters and size, complexity, and cost. Since our plasmas are relatively cool and short lived we are able to use probe based diagnostics and for the measurement of bulk plasma parameters, Langmuir probes have been chosen for this experiment.

\subsection{Basic Construction}

A Langmuir probe consists of a conductor placed in contact with the plasma. The wire is biased to a voltage applied relative to ground which we will call the probe voltage, $V_{\text{probe}}$. This will locally perturb the plasma which attracts or repels ions or electrons and thus collects a current, $I_\text{probe}$, which can be seen in figure \ref{fig:simple_langmuir_probe}. If $V_\text{probe} < V_\text{plasma}$, an electric field is formed that repels electrons and attracts ions and the inverse for $V_\text{probe} > V_\text{plasma}$.

Although these probes are relatively easy to construct, their perturbative nature leads to many difficulties in extracting the local plasma parameters. This often consists of a fitting procedure where the difference between the theoretical and experimental I-V curves is minimized.

% Additionally, many probe theories are purpose built for plasmas with no magnetic field. As we are studying magnetic reconnection in which there are in fact magnetic fields, this could change the I-V characteristic in unexpected ways. We'll discuss below why these effects are not very impactful and in future sections describe the theories we use for extracting the plasma parameters.

\subsection{Plasma Perturbation}

Since we are inserting a physical probe into the plasma, as compared to passive system like spectroscopy or an active system using lasers which are minimally perturbative, the probe itself and the electric fields generated around the probe will change the plasma that is being measured. The size of this effect and how it changes the I-V curve depends on the magnetic field.

% \subsubsection{Unmagnetized Perturbation}

Given low or no magnetic field, the probe collects nearly isotropically to an extent limited by the Debye length which creates a sheath around the probe and operates to shield the electric field caused by the difference between $V_\text{probe}$ and $V_\text{plasma}$. This length scale is very short and plasma can easily refill the sheath in this unmagnetized system. Although the sheath is perturbative, the effect drops off quickly and goes like $1 / x^2$ where $x$ is the radius of a sphere centered at the probe.

% \subsubsection{Magnetic Field Effects}

In a highly magnetized system (gyroradius far smaller than probe length scale) the plasma interacting with the probe comes from a flux tube approximately the size of the probe area projected into the magnetic field direction. Because of this, the probe can start ``emptying out'' the flux tube because of the long time scales of cross field diffusion compared to the thermal streaming along the field line. This means the extent of the probe perturbation is far longer and is localized to the flux tube dropping off like $1 / x$ where $x$ is the distance along the tube.

\subsubsection{Perturbations in our System}

Our system geometry reduces the impacts of flux tube emptying and the plasmas we are studying are often relatively unmagnetized. This is true because of geometric and magnetic field factors.

% \paragraph{Geometric Considerations}
The TREX experiment is cylindrically symmetric and our probes are placed in different toroidal planes (see figure \ref{fig:brb_cad_rendering} for coordinate system reference). This means that without any toroidal field, any emptying only occurs in the plane of the probe which may still affect the probe I-V characteristic but is not affecting other probe's measurements in a significant manner. When we do have a toroidal field, we also often have non-uniform $B_r$ and $B_z$ which means the flux tube passes through many different plasma parameters and can vary in cross-sectional area. This may help to average out any flux tube emptying because at different points along the flux tube more plasma may be able to enter the tube than just what is expected at the probe causing a reduced perturbation length.

As we have magnetic fields in our system, we do need to worry about these flux tube effects. Fortunately, our magnetic field are relatively small and near the x-point during reconnection the magnetic field is far smaller than the initial background field. We characterize this smallness by the ratio of the gyroradius to the radius of the probe area which is on the order of 5-10 in the worst case (see \ref{tab:multitip_length_scales}).

\section{Hardware}

% The BRB and TREX require certain considerations due to the machines size, at 3 m in diameter, and the duration of the experiment, which is on the order of 1 \textmu s. This has led to multiple generations of probes and in this section we will discuss the most recent developments \cite{Olson_2020}.

Our Langmuir probes must satisfy a number of requirements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Length}: The probe is attached to a port on the surface of the machine. This means it starts at a spherical radius of 1.5 m and must be inserted from there. Thus the insertable length should be fairly long and must be stable. This is described in \ref{sss:multitip_shaft_components}.
    \item \textbf{Material}: We want our probe to survive the plasma exposure and not affect our vacuum pressure via outgassing. We also don't want to have to worry about secondary electron emission. We discuss this in \ref{sss:multitip_tip_components}.
    \item \textbf{Probe Size}: We want to measure the plasma locally so our probe body needs to be small relative to the plasma gradients. It also needs to be able to fit through ball joints that can be mounted to the machine that have a radius of 2.2 cm. These considerations are taken into account in \ref{sss:multitip_tip_components}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Shaft Components}
\label{sss:multitip_shaft_components}

The probe must be able to move and reach to the center of the device (which is 3 m in diameter) while also being accurate to a few centimeters. This accuracy length scale is set by our plasma dynamics which are reproducible to the order of centimeters.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_te_probe/figures/probe_shaft_cad.png}
    \caption[*Probe Shaft CAD Diagram]{A CAD diagram of a three stage probe shaft with an outer and inner garage and the main stainless steel to alumina shaft that connects to the probe. Reproduced from \cite{Olson_2020}.}
    \label{fig:probe_shaft_cad}
\end{figure}

As can be seen in figure \ref{fig:probe_shaft_cad}, the probe shaft is a multistage system. Each stage consists of stainless steel tubing where connections between different stages are made using quick-connect high-vacuum sliding seals. We mount the probe to the machine using a flange on the outer garage or a ball joint (as seen in the figure). Then we can move each garage independently to insert the probe a desired length.

% \subsubsection{Shaft materials}

% We need to sufficiently support our probe and the shafts themselves so we use stainless steel until we enter the plasma region in which we change to alumina.

For the shafts, stainless steel is vacuum safe, tolerant to plasma interaction, and can support the weight of the structure easily without excessive flexing. Unfortunately, as a conductor, it will act as a long Langmuir probe that will create a ray in the machine at a single potential. This will perturb the plasma much more significantly than using an insulator. To that end we use alumina, a high temperature ceramic who's surface will accumulate charge such that it will be left at the floating potential (the potential at which the ion and electron currents balance). This helps to perturb the plasma less as there is no net current to the shaft and thus will be less perturbative.

At the end of the probe shaft, we use stainless steel tubing to connect from the alumina to the probe body. This is to reduce the overall size of the shaft in perturbing the plasma at the probe and extends 10 cm between alumina and probe body.

Each shaft/garage, is held in place by the sliding vacuum seal that connects it to it's larger garage. As this is only a single point of contact, we have used centering collets that allow the inner shaft/garage to move while being held in place by the outer garage. These can be both machined or 3D printed. When 3D printed we can make these to also hold the o-rings needed to seal the outer garage.

The probe shaft is at vacuum pressure and thus we attach a feedthrough to the end of the can from figure \ref{fig:probe_shaft_cad}. This feedthrough carries the plasma current hitting the probe tips between the high vacuum BRB internals to an external connector constructed using two hermetically sealed DB-25 connectors.

\subsubsection{Probe body}
\label{ss2:multitip_probe_body}

The probe body is constructed using stereolithography which gives a high resolution print necessary for the friction fit Molybdenum tips. Additionally, this plastic does not noticeable affect vacuum conditions and is resilient to the short heat fluxes received during a shot. The body must connect to two different diameter stainless steel tubes (as can be seen in figure \ref{fig:multitip_image}) and thus we use an annulus with inner/outer radii matching the two tubes. One tube connects the probe to the alumina shaft and the other connects a 3-axis B-dot coil.

\subsubsection{Tip material + size}

To collect current, we use 16 separate tips at different voltages which each sample a point on the IV curve. We make these tips out of Molybdenum which requires us to connect the tips to the signal wire using a friction fit. Secondary electron emission from molybdenum is quite small as compared to many metals \cite{Dekker_1958} which simplifies the IV-characteristic interpretation \cite{Hershkowitz_1989}. Additionally, as a refractory metal, molybdenum will not melt nor sputter at the high temperatures the probe tips may get to as they collect current.

We expect the current to the tips to be on the order of $1 \, \text{kA}/\text{m}^2$. We measure the current across a `sense' resistor (as discussed in \ref{sss:multitip_probe_circuit}) and to keep power dissipation minimal we set the size to be approximately 10 mm$^2$ such that the current is on the order of 10 mA. This amount of current is sufficiently large such that our signal-to-noise ratio is large.

We protect each tip from the base of the probe body using polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)/Teflon. Teflon does outgas in a vacuum but because of it's small size is minimally damaging and works well as an insulator.

We could potentially shorten each tip so the collecting area extends all the way to the probe body. Unfortunately because of the increased size of the body this can cause shadowing effects where tips on one side of the probe have a different IV-characteristic than those on the other. This `shadowing' is caused by plasma flow parallel to the tip direction which gets intercepted by the probe body.

Another reason to shield the tips is to avoid Mach effects that would cause different IV-characteristics for tips on opposite sides. As can be seen in figure \ref{fig:multitip_mach_effect}, in an ideal Mach probe the ions are prevented from easily accessing the ``shadowed'' side of the probe (right side) because of their inertia. If we shrink the probe body and increase the distance the tip is from the probe the Mach effect is reduced because ion inertia is not sufficient to prevent collection. This is preventative measure helps to reduce shadowing but during extreme flows may still be present. For an in depth discussion of Mach probes we refer the interested reader to \cite{Chung_2012}.

\subsection{B-dot Coils}
\label{sss:multitip_bdot}

Since we don't know the exact location of the probe and the plasma is not perfectly reproducible, it's useful to have measurements we can compare to other diagnostics to locate the probe in space and around the reconnection region. We do this using hand wound 3-axis B-dot coils that can measure the change in magnetic field near the tips.

\subsubsection{Size Considerations}

During reconnection the largest $\partial_t \vb{B}$ is on the order of 10 kT/s. Thus, from Faraday's law,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E} = \vb{A} \cdot \partial_t \vb{B}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{E}$ is the electromotive force and $\vb{A}$ is the vector area of the coil \cite{Jackson_1999}. % TODO: Check this reference.
This means that for a signal of ~1 V we are looking for a cross-sectional area of $10^{-4}$ m$^2$. This area also leads to a measured voltage that is approximately proportional to the field frequency which simplifies field reconstruction.

Using a 3D-printed B-dot body (seen at right in figure \ref{fig:multitip_image}), we wrap wire around a square shaped center six times giving an area of $\sim 5 \times 10^{-5}$ m$^2$. Although smaller than that required for a 1 V measurement, this makes sure the signal doesn't become too large should some kind of resonance exist in the circuit.

\subsubsection{Noise Reduction}

Since common mode noise can relatively easily couple to the coils, we use twisted pair for each wrapping direction. This gives us two circuits that have nearly opposite vector area, $\vb{A}_1 \approx -\vb{A}_2$, such that we can subtract the two measurements and thus remove common mode contributions.

\section{Circuitry}

The multi-tip Langmuir probe consists of three circuits used for biasing the tips, measuring the probe current, and measuring the changing magnetic field.

\subsection{Bias Source}

We have thus far had two different circuits that we've used for biasing each tip relative to the laboratory ground. The first, made by Jan Egedal while at VTF is manually controlled, while the second is remotely operated.

\subsubsection{Manually Controlled}

Our previous setup consisted of multiple electrolytic capacitors who's voltage is set by a series of resistors that split the voltage between the negative power supply ($V_-$), ground, and the positive power supply ($V_+$).

% \paragraph{Principles of operation}

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics{chapter_te_probe/figures/Old_biasing_circuit_cropped.pdf}
  \caption[*Old Multi-tip Biasing Circuit]{Old biasing circuit for the multi-tip Langmuir probe.}
  \label{fig:multitip_old_bias_circuit}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:multitip_old_bias_circuit} shows the biasing circuit simplified to a four tip configuration. The `rails' (which are the maximum and minimum voltage of the circuit) are set by power sources at $V_+$ and $V_-$ which are biased relative to the ground input connection labeled \texttt{Jgnd}. A series of resistors splits the voltage between the rails and one tip is set to laboratory ground. These three parameters let us set the maximum and minimum voltage along with the voltage difference between each positively/negatively biased tip. The voltage separation charges each tip's capacitor to a bias voltage, $V_b = \texttt{Vb}$, which exist to keep the bias voltage constant during an experimental shot.

Let $i, j \in \{1, \dots, N\}$ be the index of the tip and the index of the ground connection respectively. Then the bias voltage is
\begin{equation}
    V_{bi} = \begin{cases}
        \frac{V_-}{j} (j - i) & \text{if } j \geq i \\
        \frac{V_+}{N + 1 - j} (i - j) & \text{if } j < i
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $V_{bi}$ is the bias voltage for tip $i$.

% \paragraph{The good, the bad, and the ugly}

Since most of this circuits components are free-standing and the wired connections undergo some movement during travel and assembly, various solder joints are prone to breaking. Additionally because of the need to manually flip the polarity of each electrolytic capacitor using a switch mounted to a board and move the ground via a banana connection, it is infeasible to remotely operate the bias supply. This circuit also has no protection against setting the tip voltages extremely far apart which may cause arcing between wires or tips.

\subsubsection{Remote Controlled}
\label{ss2:multitip_remote_bias}

We've upgraded the previous setup by allowing the bias to be remotely controlled. This is done by removing the switches needed in the past setup and instead having one power supply set the voltage of the most negative tip and the other power supply set the maximum bias voltage difference between tips.

% \paragraph{Principles of operation}

This system operates by having a large capacitor that is set to some negative voltage relative to ground, $V_-$. Each tip's capacitor is biased relative to the output of a large capacitor whose voltage is $V_-$. Thus we can change the bias of all the tips by the same amount by changing the negative bias. The splitting of the bias voltage is similar to the previous setup where the voltage from the positive power supply is split using a series of resistors. The circuit schematic and images of the circuit can be seen in figures \ref{fig:multitip_new_bias_schematic} and \ref{fig:multitip_new_bias_image} respectively.

We can write out the individual tip biases mathematically as
\begin{equation}
    V_{bi} = \frac{V_+}{N + 1} i + V_-
\end{equation}
where $V_{bi}$ is the tip bias, $V_+ \geq 0$ and $V_- \leq 0$ are the applied biases, $i$ is the tip number, and $N$ is the number of tips.

% \paragraph{Problems}
During running when the probe was at low radius and $V_- = -225$ V, the positive power supply broke and a short to ground was formed internal to the power supply between the negative and ground banana connection terminals. We believe this was due to a transient effect that sent the positive power supply more than 300 V from ground which is outside the safe operating regime. Additionally the 10 W discharge resistor initially used for discharging the negative capacitor burned out because the power load was higher than expected. This has been replaced with a large carborundum resistor that can handle 50 W.

% \paragraph{Calibration}
% \label{spg:multitip_bias_calibration}
Calibration of the supply consists of two stages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The power supplies are remotely controlled through LabView which communicates with a CompactRIO analog output module that sends an isolated control voltage to the power supply. This output signal has a voltage offset and is not a one-to-one conversion between set voltage and output voltage. We can correct for these issues by setting a voltage in LabView and measuring the power supply output voltage. A linear fit is done to find the offset and multiplicative factor.
    \item Since the digitizers have an internal offset voltage and there is a resistor divider between bias and measurement we must correct for both of these effects. This is similarly a case of measuring the output bias voltage using a multimeter and correcting the digitizer measurement to match that value.
\end{itemize}

% \subsubsection{Circuit Solution}
% \label{ss2:multitip_circuit_solution}

% During a shot, noise is generated around the lab because of the high currents used to create the reversed magnetic field for reconnection. To remove this noise we use another circuit that has the same components but is not connected to a plasma facing tip which can be seen in figure \ref{fig:multitip_tip_circuit_w_noise}. We'll call this other circuit the \textbf{noise circuit} and the circuit that is connected to the tip the \textbf{signal circuit}. If there was no noise in the lab we would expect to measure no fluctuations on the noise circuit digitizer.

In order to get the IV-curve needed for extracting out the plasma parameters we need to solve for the probe voltage and current. To do this, we'll take figure \ref{fig:multitip_tip_circuit_w_noise} as our model where we have modeled the noise as a fluctuating ground voltage\footnote{We've also tried modeling the noise using a fluctuating current that appears on both the probe signal and where a probe would attach in the noise circuit. This method had reduced noise removal and thus is not described further.}. This model ignores some sources of noise from the digitizer or bias fluctuations but it works well to increase the signal-to-noise ratio throughout the shot. In solving for the probe current and voltage, the capacitor voltage has nearly no time dependence during the shot so we treat it as having a constant voltage, $V_b$.

Using Kirchhoff's laws we can construct a system of linear equations. Kirchhoff's current law gives us two equations for the nodes in the signal and noise circuits and Kirchhoff's voltage law gives six equations connecting any end node combination in each circuit. We solve these equations using \texttt{SymPy}, a symbolic math library in Python. These give the following:
\begin{align*}
  I_{ps} =& \frac{1}{R_{ss}} [(R_{ds} + R_{ss}) I_{ds} - (R_{dn} + R_{sn}) I_{dn}], \\
  V_{ps} =& \frac{1}{R_{ss}} [(R_{ds} R_{ss} + R_{ds} R_{ws} + R_{ss} R_{ws}) I_{ds} \\
  & \quad - (R_{dn} R_{ss} + R_{dn} R_{ws} + R_{sn} R_{ss} + R_{sn} R_{ws}) I_{dn}] + V_b. \\
\end{align*}
Since the digitizers are measuring the voltage output of the resistor divider we have replaced the measured voltages, $V_D$, with the currents, $I_d$, which is the total current flowing through the resistor divider. Since we know the measured voltage and the resistances of the divider and digitizer we can find the current using Fig. \ref{fig:multitip_simple_tip_circuit}:
\begin{align*}
  R_d &= R_1 + (R_2^{-1} + R_D^{-1})^{-1}, \\
  V_D &= \frac{(R_2^{-1} + R_D^{-1})^{-1}}{R_d} V_d, \\
  I_d &= \frac{V_d}{R_d}.
\end{align*}
Thus our final equations for the probe voltage and current are
\begin{align}
  I_d(V_d) =& \frac{V_d}{R_d} = \frac{V_D}{\left(R_2^{-1} + R_D^{-1}\right)^{-1}} \label{eq:multitip_I_d},\\
  I_{ps}(I_{ds}, I_{dn}) =& \frac{1}{R_{ss}} [(R_{ds} + R_{ss}) I_{ds} - (R_{dn} + R_{sn}) I_{dn}] \label{eq:multitip_I_p}, \\
  V_{ps}(I_{ds}, I_{dn}, V_b) =& \frac{1}{R_{ss}} [(R_{ds} R_{ss} + R_{ds} R_{ws} + R_{ss} R_{ws}) I_{ds} \label{eq:multitip_V_p} \\
  & \quad - (R_{dn} R_{ss} + R_{dn} R_{ws} + R_{sn} R_{ss} + R_{sn} R_{ws}) I_{dn}] + V_b \notag.
\end{align}

% \paragraph{Assumptions}

Here we have assumed that the noise is resulting from a fluctuating ground which is a reasonable assumption given that we expect significant ground fluctuations. We see fewer of these fluctuations during shots with no plasma which may mean some of this noise is due to plasma current to the probe. % It remains to be seen if there are better ways to model the common-mode noise or if we are accidentally removing bias voltage fluctuations that may be necessary for better IV-characteristic reconstructions.

% \subsubsection{Calibration}
% \label{ss2:multitip_calibration}

Since each tip's area and circuit resistances is different, we calibrate those parameters using measurements of the plasma.

% \paragraph{Calibrating in Ion Saturation Regime}

When the current to the probe is dominated by ions, the probe current is nearly independent of probe potential. By setting all tips to the same negative bias voltage we measure different currents to each tip. The ratio of measurement magnitudes between tips is proportional to the ratio of tip areas.

% \paragraph{Calibrating on the exponential}

In the ion attracting regime but near the plasma potential, the shape of the IV-characteristic is approximately exponential. During the initial equilibrium for our cold plasma ($\sim4$ eV), tips at the same bias, $V_b$, should all have nearly the same probe voltage, $V_p$. Thus, setting the tip potential to be near the plasma potential during the equilibrium plasma before the drive coils gives us the ability to calculate the wire resistance such that all the points on the IV-characteristic are at the same voltage.

% \paragraph{Bayesian calibration}

We do both of these calibrations using Bayesian statistics where we solve for the probability of the area (or resistance) based on the current to the probe. By finding the probability of the measured current for some area assuming some amount of noise and then applying a prior that forces the area to be in some reasonable range let's us find the most probable area. The same can be done for the resistance.

% \paragraph{Calibration using pressure wave}

Using negatively biased tips we calibrate the tip areas relative to one another. To calibrate the absolute tip area we use the pressure wave. Since the wave method works well to extract the initial density profile we can change the tip areas to best match those results at all radii.

Once we have the vector area of each coil, $\vb{A}_i$, we can now extract the B-dot signal. According to Faraday's law,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}_i = \vb{A}_i\cdot\dot{\vb{B}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{E}_i$ is the voltage across the coil leads. Now we measure a signal, $S_i$, where
\begin{equation}
    S_i = \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \mathcal{E}_i.
\end{equation}
Assuming the vector areas are non-singular, this can be written as
\begin{align}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        S_1 \\
        S_2 \\
        \vdots \\
        S_6
    \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix}
        \vb{A}_1 \\
        \vb{A}_2 \\
        \vdots \\
        \vb{A}_6 \\
    \end{bmatrix} \cdot \dot{\vb{B}} \\
    \vb{S} &= \vb{A} \cdot \dot{\vb{B}}.
\end{align}
Now $\dot{\vb{B}}$ is overdetermined since we have six measurements and three field components so using linear least squares\footnote{We've also tried using generalized least squares (since coils made of the same twisted pair may have more correlated noise) but we did not see a significant improvement.} we can solve for $\dot{\vb{B}}$ and also the standard deviation of $\dot{\vb{B}}$. This is useful in understanding where we might expect error in our signals and whether we need to smooth signals to get rid of noise caused by the firing of the capacitor banks (which is sometimes needed).

\section{Plasma Theory}

There are many theories that have been developed and applied to Langmuir probes. Having applied a number of potential theories to our results we find that two theories stand out.

\subsection{Simple Plasma Theory (Hutchinson)}
\label{sss:multitip_hutchinson_theory}

\cite{Hutchinson_2002} develops a simple model for the current to a Langmuir probe that has in the past been used for analysis. This theory assumes that the ion current is constant and the electron current follows an exponential while only being valid for the $V_p < V_b$ regime.

% \subsubsection{Derivation}
% \label{ss2:multitip_hutchinson_derivation}

% We won't reproduce the derivation here but will instead describe the physical interpretation.

The volume surrounding the probe is split into two regions: \textbf{the plasma} where the electric field is very small, quasineutrality holds, and the potential is near the plasma potential vs. \textbf{the sheath} where the ion electron charge densities are different and the electric field is large such that the potential quickly drops to the probe potential.

The ion current is modeled by how many ions randomly cross the plasma-sheath interface (the interface will be denoted by subscript $s$). If the ions are cold (i.e. negligible initial kinetic energy) then they will only get to the probe via their random motion. The speed of these ions is set by the voltage at which the interface appears which is where
\begin{equation}
    V_s = -\frac{T_e}{2}.
\end{equation}
$V_s$ is the potential at the interface relative to the plasma potential and $T_e$ is in units of eV. Assuming the electrons are Boltzmann and quasineutrality holds at the interface then
\begin{equation}
    n_{is} = n_{es} = n_\infty \exp\left(\frac{V_s}{T_e}\right).
\end{equation}
Now, the ion current is
\begin{align}
    I_i &= A_s v_{is} n_{is} \\
    &= A_s \sqrt{-\frac{2eV_s}{m_i}} n_\infty \exp\left(\frac{V_s}{T_e}\right) \\
    &= \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) A_s n_\infty \sqrt{\frac{e T_e}{m_i}}.
\end{align}
We assume that the sheath area is the same as the probe area since the Debye length is so short in TREX.

For the electron current, we assume electrons are Boltzmann and will hit the probe due to random motion. Since they are repelled by the probe the density will be smaller than in the bulk plasma. We can write the electron current as
\begin{equation}
    I_e = A_p \frac{1}{4} \sqrt{\frac{2 e T_e}{m_e}} n_\infty \exp\left(\frac{e (V_\text{probe} - V_\text{plasma})}{T_e}\right)
\end{equation}
where the square root is the average electron velocity and the exponential is the factor the electron density is reduced.

The total current to the probe for $V_\text{probe} < V_\text{plasma}$ is thus
\begin{equation}
    I = -n_\infty e A_p \sqrt{\frac{e T_e}{m_i}} \left[ \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{\frac{2 m_i}{\pi m_e}} \exp\left(\frac{V_\text{probe} - V_\text{plasma}}{T_e}\right) - \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right] % TODO: Where does the pi come from?
    \label{eq:multitip_hutchinson_model}
\end{equation}
where we have chosen the convention of ion current to the probe being positive.

% \subsubsection{Pros \& Cons}

This method has the benefit of being simple to implement and applicable to many plasmas. As long as the tip voltage is below the plasma potential this usually matches our measurements. During TREX, the plasma potential can change quickly by up to 100 or more volts and thus tips are sometimes in a valid regime for analysis and other times not. The user can manually select probe tips at each time point that appear as though they are below the plasma potential but this is both time consuming and prone to human error.

\subsection{BRL}
\label{sss:multitip_brl_theory}

\cite{Laframboise_1966} created a model for the current to a cylindrical probe in a plasma with Maxwellian ions and electrons that works for probe voltages above and below the plasma potential where collisions between particles are ignored. The ion and electron distribution functions are found self consistently and iteratively evolved until convergence. This theory requires some simplification to be numerically tractable for application to probe data.

For probe potential greater than plasma potential, i.e. electrons attracted, we follow along with appendix F of \cite{Laframboise_1966}. We assume that in TREX the ions have zero temperature. Additionally, we assume we assume that the probe radius is far larger than the Debye length. This approximation is valid for the initial plasma but can change throughout the shot. We also assume that all particles that touch the probe are absorbed by it.

Since the scale over which the potential changes is proportional to the Debye length, we assume we have a planar probe. Thus Poisson's equation is given by
\begin{equation}
  \dv[2]{\varphi}{x} = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon}
  \label{eq:planar_poissons}
\end{equation}

Since the ions have zero temperature, they can not enter the $\varphi(x) > \varphi_0$ region and thus for the plasma charge density, $\rho(x)$, they can be ignored. Without loss of generality we'll set the plasma potential, $\varphi_0$, equal to 0. Since this region of electron only plasma physically can not extend to infinity we define $x = 0$ to be this boundary and let $x$ increase towards the probe.

This is a one-dimensional system so we have three conserved quantities. $v_y$ and $v_z$ which denote the transverse velocity will not be changed by the potential and the kinetic energy in the $x$ direction along with the electrostatic potential in eV, $E_x = m / e v_x^2/2 + \varphi(x)$. $e$ is the elementary charge. As all electrons will be absorbed by the probe, we will only have flow into the $\varphi(x) > 0$ region. Thus our distribution function is given by
\begin{equation}
  f(x, E_x, E_t) = \begin{cases}
    n_0 \left(\frac{m e}{2\pi T}\right)^{3/2} \exp(-\frac{E_x(x)}{T}) \exp(-\frac{E_t}{T}) & v_x > 0 \\
    0 & v_x < 0
  \end{cases}.
  \label{eq:planar_distribution_function}
\end{equation}
We can now write the density as
\begin{align*}
  n(x) =& \int_0^\infty dv_x \int_{-\infty}^\infty dv_y \int_{-\infty}^\infty dv_z f(x, E_x, E_t) \\
  =& \int_0^\infty \dv{v_x}{E_x} dE_x \int_{-\infty}^\infty dv_y \int_{-\infty}^\infty dv_z f(x, E_x, E_t) \\
  =& n_0 \left(\frac{m e}{2\pi T}\right)^{3/2} \int_0^\infty \frac{\exp(-\frac{E_x(x)}{T})}{\sqrt{2m (E_x - \varphi(x))}} dE_x \\
  & \int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp(-\frac{m e v_y^2}{2T}) dv_y \int_{-\infty}^\infty \exp(-\frac{m e v_z^2}{2T}) dv_z \\
  =& n_0 \sqrt{\frac{m e}{2\pi T}} \int_0^\infty \frac{\exp(-\frac{E_x(x)}{T})}{\sqrt{2m (E_x - \varphi(x))}} dE_x.
\end{align*}
Let $\eta = n(x) / n_0$ where $n_0$ is the number density at infinity, $\chi(x) = \varphi(x) / T$ is the normalized potential, and $\beta = E_x / T$ is the normalized energy. Thus we can write the normalized density as
\begin{align}
  \eta(x) &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{\exp(-\beta)}{\sqrt{\beta - \chi(x)}} d\beta \nonumber \\
  &= \frac{e^{-\chi(x)}}{2} (1 - \erf(\sqrt{-\chi(x)})) \label{eq:planar_normalized_density}.
\end{align}
Using our now normalized quantities and Poisson's equation \ref{eq:planar_poissons} we find
\begin{align}
  \frac{\varepsilon kT}{e}\dv[2]{\chi}{x} &= e \eta(x) \nonumber \\
  \lambda_D^2 \dv[2]{\chi}{x} &= \eta(\chi(x)) \nonumber \\
  \dv[2]{\chi}{s} &= \eta(\chi(s))
  \label{eq:planar_normalized_poissons}
\end{align}
where $s = x / \lambda_D$ and $\lambda_D = \sqrt{\varepsilon T / n_0 e}$ is the Debye length in the undisturbed plasma.

Since the potential is zero at the sheath boundary and the derivative is smooth, $\chi(0) = 0$ and $\dv*{\chi}{s}(0) = 0$. For all $s > 0$, the density of electrons should be non-zero, i.e. we should never achieve a full vacuum except at infinity. Thus,
\begin{equation*}
  \dv[2]{\chi}{s}(s) > 0, \, \dv{\chi}{s}(0) = 0 \Rightarrow \dv{\chi}{s}(s) > 0 \Rightarrow \left(\dv{\chi}{s}\right)^{-1} = \dv{s}{\chi}.
\end{equation*}
This implies
\begin{align*}
  \dv[2]{\chi}{s} &= \dv{}{s} \left(\dv{s}{\chi}\right)^{-1} \\
  &= \dv{\chi}{s} \dv{}{\chi} \left(\dv{s}{\chi}\right)^{-1} \\
  \eta(\chi) &= -\left(\dv{s}{\chi}\right)^{-3} \dv[2]{s}{\chi}\\
  \Rightarrow \int_{0}^{\chi} \eta(\chi') d\chi' &= \frac{1}{2} \left(\dv{s}{\chi}\right)^{-2} \\
  \Rightarrow \dv{s}{\chi} &= \frac{1}{2 \int_{0}^{\chi} \eta(\chi') d\chi'} \\
  \Rightarrow s(\chi) &= \int_{0}^{\chi} \frac{d\chi'}{2 \int_{0}^{\chi'} \eta(\chi'') d\chi''}.
\end{align*}
Thus we can pass a probe potential, $\chi$, and after integrating get the distance from the probe to the start of the sheath.

Since all particles that enter the sheath are collected, from equation \ref{eq:planar_distribution_function}, then we need only find how the surface area of the sheath changes for different potentials. Letting $I_0$ be the current collected from the electrons when the probe is at the plasma potential then the current to the probe is
\begin{equation}
  I(\chi) = I_0 \left(1 + \frac{\lambda_D}{R_p} s(\chi)\right)^2.
  \label{eq:planar_probe_current}
\end{equation}

Now that we have a model for the current to the probe for the electron attracted region, we move on to modeling the current to the probe for the ion attracted region. Compared to the electron attracting regime, there has been much more work done to parametrize \cite{Laframboise_1966}. \cite{Steinbrüchel_1990} and \cite{Karamcheti_Steinbrüchel_1999} fit a model of the form
\begin{equation*}
  I = I_{i0} a (-\chi)^b + I_{e0} \exp(\chi) \quad \chi < 0
\end{equation*}
where $a$ and $b$ were fit to the data given by Laframboise. \cite{Mausbach_1997} used
\begin{equation*}
  I = \begin{cases}
    I_{i0} a_i (b_i - \chi)^{c_i} + I_{e0} \exp(\chi) \quad & \chi < 0 \\
    I_{e0} a_e (b_e + \chi)^{c_e} \quad & \chi \geq 0
  \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
where $a_{i,e}$, $b_{i,e}$, and $c_{i,e}$ are fitted parameters for the ions and electrons. 

Unfortunately neither of these correctly predict the ion current to the probe for large $\chi$ so we instead use the parametrization given by Chen \cite{Chen_2001} which better predicts ion current for $\chi < 0$. The total current to the probe is given by
\begin{align*}
  I &= I_{i0} i(\chi) + I_{e0} \exp(\chi) \\
\frac{1}{i^4} &= \frac{1}{\left(A \chi^B\right)^4} + \frac{1}{\left(C \chi^D\right)^4}
\end{align*}
where $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ are functions of $\xi \equiv R_p / \lambda_D$. We will reproduce the work from Chen here for sake of completeness

$ABCD$ are defined in two different regimes. For large $\xi > 3$,
\begin{align*}
  A, B, D(\xi) &= a + b(\xi-c)^d \exp[-f(\xi-c)^g] \\
  C &= a + b \exp[-c \ln(\xi - d)] + f(1-g \ln \xi).
\end{align*}
This is accurate to within 3\%.

Although not as accurate but useful for all $\xi$, we have
\begin{align*}
  A &= a + \frac{1}{\frac{1}{b \xi^c} - \frac{1}{d \ln(\xi / f)}} \\
  B, D &= a + b \xi^c \exp(-d\xi^f) \\
  C &= a + b \xi^{-c}
\end{align*}

The values for $a,\,b,\,c,\,d,\,f,$ and $g$ are given in table \ref{tab:chen_values}.

\subsection{Effect of electric fields}

Electric fields during reconnection can change the average plasma potential all of the tips see which is extracted by fitting the plasma theory to the tip measurements. The effect of these electric fields is non-local because the measured plasma potential is integrated along the path of the probe shaft. Electric fields may also have a local effect directly measurable by the difference in measurement between tips on one side of the probe vs. the other (see figure \ref{fig:multitip_image}).

% During reconnection we expect the magnetic field to be quickly changing along the axis of the machine as the $z$ directed field reconnects. Since we are dealing with a plasma we expect small static electric fields because of quasineutrality. Also, since the system is toroidally periodic there can be no net static field in the $\phi$ direction.

% \paragraph{Inductive Fields}

Inductive electric fields are generated by the change in magnetic field as the experiment progresses. A change in a directed magnetic field leads to the following effects:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $z$: This is the field that changes most during the experiment because it is reconnecting. Because of toroidal symmetry, the most general closed loop we can construct are annulus arcs with electric fields in the $r$ and $\phi$ directions. Because of toroidal symmetry the changing $z$ field will create a negligible $r$ directed electric field but can create toroidal electric fields (the drive coils generate an immense toroidal electric field to drive reconnection).
    \item $r$: This field can create either a $z$ or $\phi$ directed electric field. The $\phi$ electric field can be generated but, because of symmetry, the closed loop on the boundary of a cylinder arc causes $E_z$ to be minimal. One other detriment to $E_z$ is the small magnitudes of $B_r$ which can form in the outflow region of reconnection but are often smaller than $B_z$.
    \item $\phi$: The source of $\phi$ field can come from two places: a guide field that is frozen into the plasma (see \ref{sss:experiment_timeline}) or the hall fields when the ions and electrons decouple (see \ref{ss2:hall_reconnection}). This allows for the formation of both $r$ and $z$ electric fields but because of the often small magnitude of $B_\phi$ we don't expect large field generation.
\end{itemize}

Since our plasmas are often quasineutral, static electric fields should be small. Additionally, because of toroidal symmetry there can be no net static electric field in the $\phi$ direction because if $E_\phi \neq 0$ this would force the electrostatic potential to be multivalued which is physically impossible.

As discussed in \ref{ss2:intro_PA_generation}, a trapping potential forms that changes the shape of the flux tube as it moves towards the x-point during reconnection. This potential is on the order of a few $T_e$ \cite{Egedal_Daughton_Drake_Katz_Lê_2009} which gives an electric field in our device on the order of 100 V/m. Note that this trapping potential forms to create electric fields in the $rz$-plane.

During the pressure wave, the low inertia of the electrons let's them speed up far faster than the ions. This acceleration occurs as the wavefront passes plasma at a given radius which generates an ambipolar % TODO: Check I'm using ambipolar right here
electric field in the $+r$ direction that slows the electrons and accelerates ions to enforce quasineutrality.

\subsection{Minimum measurable density}
\label{sss:multitip_min_density}

As the plasma density drops at higher radii, the Debye length increases such that the electric fields around neighboring probes may begin to overlap.

% \subsubsection{What's the Debye Length}

% The Debye length is the distance at which electric fields are shielded in a plasma and goes as
% \begin{equation}
%     \lambda_D = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_0 T_e}{n e}}.
%     \label{eq:debye_length}
% \end{equation}
% Electric fields drop exponentially like $E = A \exp(x / \lambda_D)$ because the electrons and ions are somewhat free to move and will quickly remove any static electric field. Inductive fields on the other hand stick around and instead cause the plasma and it's frozen in magnetic field to move.

% \subsubsection{Sheath scale}

As discussed in \ref{sss:multitip_hutchinson_theory}, a Langmuir probe can be modeled using a sheath-plasma interface where quasineutrality holds in the plasma but electrons and ions separate inside the sheath and electric fields become large. Although not all Langmuir theories use this model (e.g. BRL), this interface is usually present to some approximation. The distance at which this sheath appears is often on the order of 10 $\lambda_D$.

% \subsubsection{Tip-tip sheath overlap}

If we have sheaths of neighboring tips that overlap (either because of high temperature of low density) this could change the IV-characteristic that we measure. Often once we have high temperatures the density is high enough such that the Debye length is far smaller than the tip separation (see table \ref{tab:multitip_length_scales}) so we will not consider those effects here. Early in the shot when the density can be low, the sheaths can start to overlap. Assuming the sheath length is $20 \times \lambda_D$, this gives a minimum measurable density of
\begin{align}
    x &= 20 \lambda_D \\
    &= 20 \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_0 T_e}{n_\text{min} e}} \\
    \Rightarrow n_\text{min} &= \left(\frac{20}{x}\right)^2 \frac{\epsilon_0 T_e}{e} \\
    &\approx 2e-16 \text{ m}^{-3}
\end{align}
where we've set $T_e = 4$ eV which is often what is seen in the initial equilibrium and $x$ is the distance between tips.

\section{Curve Fitting}

Once we have measurements of the probe voltage and current we can extract the plasma parameters. This is done using a fitting routine that compares a model of the Langmuir probe response to the data measured by the probe.

Plasma parameters are commonly fit by subtracting off the ion current in the ion attracted regime and then fitting the exponential in log space using a straight line. This works quite well in swept Langmuir probe applications where many points exist along the IV-characteristic and there signal-to-noise ratio is very large. Our system doesn't fit either of these criteria so we have used some alternative methods.

% \subsection{Least squares Fitting}

Least squares has often been used in our lab because of it's simplicity. This fitting routine operates by minimizing the squared error in $J$ between the function to fit, $J(V)$, and the samples, $(V_i, J_i)$.

% \subsubsection{Benefits}

Least squares is very quick to solve and thus analyzing the IV-characteristic during the entire shot is very quick. Additionally, it is extremely easy to implement as many programming language offer this as a pre-written method.

% \subsubsection{Issues}

This method does dome with a number of drawbacks. Since it is only minimizing error in the $J$ direction it does not account for errors in measurement that also cause errors in $V$. This is especially problematic when using Hutchinson's model and the probe is collecting in the electron saturation region. Since Hutchinson's model does not account for this component it artificially raises the electron temperature and this is especially visible when only minimizing the error in $J$. Least squares also often uses a simple method for finding a `best fit'. This means that if the fit starts with a bad initial guess it may end at a suboptimal fit.

% \subsection{ODR}
% \label{sss:multitip_odr}

To correctly model the errors in our circuit we use orthogonal distance regression (implemented by \cite{Boggs_Byrd_Rogers_Schnabel}) which allows us to model the errors we expect to have in our measurements of the probe current and voltage.

% \subsubsection{What does error look like in our signals}

We use three different measurements for calculating the probe current and voltage and multiple parameters that we set for the circuit (circuit resistances and tip areas). Although these parameters are derived from data and are not exact, we don't account for errors in them and instead set them to their calibrated values.

Our measured parameters are $V_{ds}$, $V_{dn}$, and $V_b$ which can be seen in equations \ref{eq:multitip_I_d}-\ref{eq:multitip_V_p}. Errors in our $V_b$ measurement will only cause errors in $V_p$ whereas $V_{ds}$ and $V_{dn}$ will cause shifts mostly along a line given by
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta_I = \frac{R_d + R_s}{R_d R_s + R_d R_w + R_s R_w} \Delta_V.
\end{equation*}

% \subsubsection{How does ODR take that into account}

Instead of passing the final values to fit, $(V_i, J_i)$, orthogonal distance regression (ODR) uses the raw measurements, $(V_{Dsi}, V_{Dni}, V_{bi})$. Then we fit the model defined by
\begin{align*}
    0 &\approx f(n, T_e, V_p; \vb{V}_{Ds}, \vb{V}_{Dn}, \vb{V}_{b}) \\
    &= J_p^\text{theory}(V_p^\text{circuit}(\vb{V}_{Ds}, \vb{V}_{Dn}, \vb{V}_{b}), n, T_e, V_p) - J_p^\text{circuit}(\vb{V}_{Ds}, \vb{V}_{Dn}).
\end{align*}
Here the superscript defines whether we are using the plasma model (which depends on the plasma parameters to fit) or the circuit model (which depends on the circuit parameters and measurements).

Now as ODR tries to fit this model, it'll allow error in all of our measured values which correctly reconstructs the errors we might expect from our measurements.

% \subsubsection{How to apply parameter bounds}

To reduce spurious solutions we want to set bounds on the various plasma parameters and remove potentially unphysical solutions like negative temperatures and densities. More recent iterations of ODR have methods of applying bounds \cite{Zwolak_et_al_2007}. Unfortunately because of dependency issues between MDSPlus (our experiments data storage system) and \href{https://pypi.org/project/odrpack/}{\texttt{odrpack}} we use a different method for applying bounds.

% \paragraph{Why apply bounds}

% \paragraph{Our method}

We know that physically the density and temperature should both be greater than zero. An easy way to do this is to instead fit the logarithm of those parameters. Then, no matter the final fit value, when converted to a physical number these parameters will be greater than zero.

To remove bad fits when the temperature or density may exceed expected limits, we may set both upper and lower bounds. To do this we fit a parameter $x$ for the plasma parameter $y$ where
\begin{equation*}
    y = y_\text{min} + (y_\text{max} - y_\text{min}) \left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\left(x\right)\right).
\end{equation*}
Thus no matter the final fit value of $x$, the plasma parameter stays within the set bounds.

Both ODR and least squares use local minimizers that attempt to reduce the error between the model and the data as much as possible. As can be seen in figure \ref{fig:multitip_example_fit}, the dashed blue lines are `local' optimums to the fitting function. That is, the fitting function has converged to a solution and is done fitting. As can be seen from the figure some of those solutions are not representative of the measured data.

% \subsubsection{A simple solution}

Many of these local minima are due to bad initial guesses so we fit each time point many times using many different initial parameter guesses. Although some may converge to solutions that don't fit the data, some will match very well and using the most similar solution should give us the most accurate plasma parameters.

% \subsubsection{How do we sample initial conditions}

To choose initial guesses we use the fitting result for the previous time point as the plasma differentiably evolved from this prior state. Assuming the plasma has changed some amount from this state (i.e. the density could be an order of magnitude smaller or an order of magnitude higher), we set parameter bounds on initial guesses for $n$, $T_e$, and $V_\text{plasma}$. Having chosen a number of starting points $N$ we use latin hypercube sampling which samples this bounded rectangle of possibilities uniformly.
